Yes. Once you've got randomly positioned cubes, you can create an "Interstellar" or deep-space feel by changing how they move, look, and how the camera behaves rather than just adding more cubes.

Some ideas to experiment with:

Slow rotation: Give every cube a different rotation speed so they don't all spin identically.
Different sizes: Randomize each cube's scale. Tiny cubes mixed with large ones make the scene feel more natural.
Spread them farther apart: Instead of a range like -5 to 5, try much larger ranges (e.g. hundreds of units) so the world feels vast.
Move the camera: Slowly fly the camera through the field of cubes. This often has a bigger visual impact than moving the cubes themselves.
Dark background: Use a black or very dark blue scene background.
Fog: Add fog so distant cubes fade away, creating depth.
Lighting: Use subtle ambient light with one directional or point light instead of very bright lighting.
Emissive colors: Give some cubes a faint glow or use bloom post-processing for a sci-fi look.
Stars: Instead of only cubes, add thousands of tiny points (particles) as stars.
Floating motion: Make cubes gently drift using Math.sin() and Math.cos() with different offsets, as if they're suspended in space.
Random orientations: Give each cube a random initial rotation so they don't all start aligned.

If you're inspired specifically by Interstellar's tesseract scene, you could also:

Create layers of cubes extending far into the distance.
Use a monochrome palette (black, white, gray, with subtle blue highlights).
Add bloom and depth-of-field effects.
Move the camera slowly through the structure instead of making everything move quickly.

A nice next challenge after your current exercise would be to create a 3D starfield: thousands of small particles with your cubes floating among them while the camera slowly flies forward. That combination gives a cinematic space feeling.
